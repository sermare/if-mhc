\noindent\textbf{The problem.} TCR-engineering therapies promise a transformative future for cancer
treatment, and with the 2024 approval of afamitresgene autoleucel that future has begun to
arrive\footnote{Singh, Mol.\ Ther.\ 32:3195, 2024.}. Yet cross-reactivity remains a fundamental barrier
to scaling them into personalized medicine. The danger is not hypothetical. An affinity-enhanced MAGE-A3
receptor that had cleared extensive preclinical testing caused fatal cardiac toxicity in two
patients\footnote{Linette et al., Cardiovascular toxicity and titin cross-reactivity of affinity-enhanced
T cells. Blood 122:863--871, 2013.}, cross-recognizing an unrelated self-peptide from the muscle protein
titin that was identified only after the fact\footnote{Cameron et al., A titin-derived HLA-A1 peptide as
cross-reactive target for MAGE-A3 T cells. Sci.\ Transl.\ Med.\ 5:197ra103, 2013.}.

\noindent\textbf{Why the field is stuck.} The bottleneck has never been imagination, it has been
throughput. Experimental validation is slow and costly, an untenable price when the presented peptide
space is astronomically large, on the order of $10^{11}$, and the fast-growing tumors these therapies
target leave only a narrow clinical window to act. The extreme polymorphism of HLA leaves binding data
sparse for most alleles and for underrepresented populations, precisely where personalized therapy is
needed most. Amino-acid scanning resolves one receptor at a time and does not scale to the combinatorics
of the peptide--TCR interaction.

\noindent\textbf{The approach.} The tools to test this at scale have only now come within reach. Two
model classes make it possible. Diffusion models generate backbones by iteratively denoising random
coordinates into a structure consistent with a supplied context. Inverse folding solves the converse
problem, encoding each residue's geometric environment as a graph and decoding the sequence most
compatible with it, learned without any binding measurement\footnote{Ribeiro-Filho et al.,
Structure-based deep learning for TCR design. PLOS Comput.\ Biol., 2024.}. I would bring two
complementary tools of my own design built on them. ESMCBA is a binding-affinity system built on protein
language models with domain adaptation that recovers usable signal even for data-scarce MHC
alleles\footnote{Mares, Espinoza, Ioannidis, Domain-specific pre-training of protein language models for
pMHC-I binding. PMLR 311:304--325, 2025.}. The second uses diffusion models conditioned on the MHC groove
to sample compatible peptides across high-priority alleles\footnote{Mares, Espinoza, Ioannidis,
Structure-guided pMHC-I libraries using diffusion models. ICML GenAI Biology Wksp, 2025.}. Together they
let me computationally screen these interactions and chart the cross-reactive neighborhood of a receptor
before it ever reaches a patient.

\noindent\textbf{How it is proven.} Each prediction is falsifiable at the bench. The receptor either
engages the flagged peptide or it does not. The scoring criterion comes first. Holding MHC and receptor
fixed and leaving the peptide de novo, generation is conditioned only on receptor-side contacts, sweeping
their number to locate the breadth at which a target conformation becomes reachable. Each backbone is
scored on three criteria that must agree, proximity to target, occupancy of the B and F anchor pockets,
and burial depth, with thresholds calibrated against native molecular-dynamics ensembles of both
conformations so the envelope has a defined width rather than an arbitrary cutoff
(Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}). Threading direction is checked explicitly, since a reversed chain scores well
on deviation alone. In parallel, four inverse-folding models applied to twenty solved pMHC--TCR
structures, with the receptor present and deleted, identify the positions whose recovery collapses on
deletion, which are the positions that receptor reads. These are validated against measured mutations
from SKEMPI and IEDB matched by CDR3, and an architecture-matched variant retrained without MHC and TCR
structures separates learned chemistry from memorization. Validation then proceeds in two stages.
Retrospectively, the screen runs blind against receptors whose off-targets are documented and must
recover them, with MAGE-A3 as the primary test, since the titin epitope is known and was missed by the
methods then in use. A screen that fails to flag it fails outright. Prospectively, top-ranked candidates
go to binding measurement and, for binders, to functional assay, yielding what the field lacks, a
false-negative rate on peptides never seen in training. The loop closes within the appointment.

\noindent\textbf{What becomes possible.} A screen that runs in silico before infusion turns
cross-reactivity from a lethal surprise into a predictable and testable step, applied to every candidate
receptor rather than the few that fit an experimental budget, and making the next generation of cancer
therapies measurably safer. It generalizes to any therapy built on engineered molecular recognition,
where the failure mode is likewise specificity, not potency. The societal stake is measured in patients,
not benchmarks.

\noindent\textbf{Trajectory.} The first problem is chosen because it is winnable inside a year, the
second because it is worth a career. Near term the deliverable is the validated screen and its
false-negative rate. Beyond it lies whether specificity can be designed at the outset, not audited
after.

\noindent Recent unpublished work of my own indicates both routes are tractable and that the
discrimination criterion behaves as intended on real designs, which is what makes this worth building
now.
