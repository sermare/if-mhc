\section{Motivation}

TCR-engineering therapies promise a transformative future for cancer treatment, yet cross-reactivity
remains a fundamental barrier to scaling them into personalized medicine. The danger is not hypothetical:
an affinity-enhanced MAGE-A3 receptor that had cleared extensive preclinical testing caused fatal cardiac
toxicity in two patients, cross-recognizing an unrelated self-peptide from the muscle protein titin that
was identified only after the fact.

Three problems keep this liability beyond the reach of current methods. First, experimental validation is
slow and costly, an untenable price when the presented peptide space is astronomically large (on the
order of $10^{11}$, roughly the number of stars in the Milky Way) and the fast-growing tumors these
therapies target leave only a narrow clinical window to act. Second, the extreme polymorphism of HLA
leaves binding data sparse for most alleles and for underrepresented populations, precisely where
personalized therapy is needed most. Third, amino-acid scanning resolves one receptor at a time and does
not scale to the combinatorics of the peptide--TCR interaction.

\section{Proposed Framework}

I would bring two complementary tools of my own design to this problem: \textbf{ESMCBA}, a
binding-affinity system built on protein language models with domain adaptation that recovers usable
signal even for data-scarce MHC alleles (Mares, Espinoza, and Ioannidis 2025a); and a
\textbf{structure-guided generative framework} that uses diffusion models conditioned on the MHC groove
to sample compatible peptides across high-priority alleles (Mares, Espinoza, and Ioannidis 2025b).
Together they let me computationally screen these interactions and chart the cross-reactive neighborhood
of a receptor before it ever reaches a patient.

Making that screen trustworthy requires an evaluation standard the field lacks. Generative backbone
models are scored by deviation to a single crystal structure, which cannot separate a design genuinely
matching a target conformation from one merely close to the groove; sequence models are summarized by one
average recovery rate, obscuring which constraints produce the signal. I propose that every claim instead
be evaluated by discrimination between two structurally defined alternatives
(Figure~\ref{fig:framework}).

\paragraph{Aim 1: Backbone conformation.} Cross-reactive TCRs engage chemically distinct peptides through
distinct bound conformations, giving a natural two-state system in which off-target recognition is
structurally explicit. I will ask whether a generative model, given only receptor-side contacts and no
peptide template, can be steered to a target conformation, including deliberately to the conformation
\emph{other} than the one nearest its conditioning input. Thresholds will be calibrated against native
molecular-dynamics ensembles rather than a single snapshot, and independent criteria (proximity, anchor
identity, burial depth) must agree.

\paragraph{Aim 2: Sequence constraints.} Inverse-folding models recover native TCR interface residues
well above chance (Ribeiro-Filho et al.\ 2024), but on the receptor side. I will apply them to the
\emph{peptide} across a panel of solved pMHC--TCR structures, with the TCR present and computationally
removed, to identify which positions a receptor actually reads. Those positions define the axes along
which a cross-reactive partner can differ, and therefore where a safety screen must look.

\section{Expected Outcomes}

This fellowship would let me develop that screen into a routine safeguard, turning cross-reactivity from
a lethal surprise into a predictable and testable step, and making the next generation of cancer
therapies measurably safer.
